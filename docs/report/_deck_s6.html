Chapter VII · where we stand

One level down: it has two kinds of decision, and only one is safe

Split every confident commit in two. A confirmation agrees with the drafter. An override overrules it. On 8,687 held-out pass-1 slots the two behave nothing alike.

Why it ties. Confirmations cannot exceed the drafter's 55.5%, and the judge is confident on only 62% of the correct drafts it sees. The decisions that would break that ceiling are the overrides, the 17%-wrong ones. Two caps, and the model is pinned between them.
Chapter VII · where we stand

The errors are single tokens in the answer wrapper

The judge's prose is indistinguishable from the stock model's — the same reasoning steps, occasionally a different word. The failures are not sloppy thinking. They are one bad override in the last few tokens.

stock → \boxed{180}$. judge → \boxed{180$. missing brace stock → $\boxed{64}$ judge → boxed{64}$ missing backslash

Correct reasoning, correct number, wrong wrapper, marked wrong. At τ = .95, 2 or 3 of the 9 wrong answers are this. Under confirm .90 / override .99 they vanish entirely.

Encouraging, not damning. A model that broke the reasoning would have broken judgement. A model that breaks a brace has broken calibration at a handful of high-stakes slots. That is a threshold and data problem.

On a task where one token flips the score, override precision matters far more than average token accuracy. The asymmetric rule exists because of this.

Stock and the free rule never break the format. They only fill slots the big model was already sure of. The judge is the first decoder that replaces a token, and replacement is where the risk lives.
