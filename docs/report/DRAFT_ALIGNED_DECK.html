Chapter I · the machine

What one pass actually costs, from memory traffic

A mixture-of-experts layer only runs 8 experts per slot, but 32 slots choose independently, so the layer must fetch the weights of their union. That union, not the top-8, is the cost.

bytes per MoE forward = 58 experts × 48 layers × 3 matrices × 768 × 2048 × 2 bytes ≈ 26 GB + ~3 GB dense drafter 1.7B, bf16 = 3.4 GB one drafter forward ≈ 1/8.5 of a 30B pass

So the honest unit is the expert-layer execution: one expert, at one layer, in one pass. Divide by tokens produced and you get a number that does not depend on our kernels.

Chapter I · the machine

Why nine passes: a marginal is flat where a conditional is peaked

The block does not take nine passes because the model is unsure what to say. It takes nine passes because of which question a masked slot is being asked.

A slot whose token is forced by the prefix — the second digit of 10 × 2 = 20 — gets 0.99 and commits at once. Everything phrasable stalls. Each commit forces about three more slots, which commit next pass, which force three more. That chain is the nine passes.

Chapter I · the machine

The waste is measurable, and it is a third of the block

The model is not wrong at those slots. It is insufficiently sure of something it already has right, because the marginal is spread over paraphrases.

The whole opportunity in one sentence. If something supplies one concrete completion, a third of the block turns from "flat and stalled" into "peaked and committable" without the model changing its mind about anything.

Recomputed on CPU from the recorded stock traces (620 blocks, 100 prompts). Earlier drafts of this deck said 7.7 of 29 and 60%; the correct figures are 7.5 of 29.4 and 58.4%.

Chapter II · the idea

Per slot, not per prefix

Be careful with the size of this advantage. Textbook speculative decoding assumes each slot is an independent coin, which at 56% gives an accepted prefix of about 2 tokens. Measured here it is not: agreement is one shared trajectory, and the run of identical tokens from the start of a block averages 13.1 slots (median 9). So per-slot buys roughly 18 of 32 against ~13, not against 2. The real edge is that it also takes the correct slots scattered after a divergence, which a prefix scheme cannot reach at any agreement rate.

The price of that freedom: a causal verifier is free and exact, because the big model's output at position i is already a judgement of i+1 made without seeing it. A bidirectional block model has no such position. There is no free verifier here.

Chapter III · the obstacle

The stock model physically cannot read a filled slot

SDAR's training loss is computed only at masked slots — that is what masked diffusion means. The output logits at a slot that already holds a token were never touched by a single gradient step.

So what answers there? The base model underneath is Qwen3, an ordinary next-token predictor, and that behaviour survives untouched at exactly the positions diffusion training never visited.

Reading a filled slot returns a shifted copy of the input. Not a weak judgement, not a noisy one. A different function entirely. This one fact explains every earlier attempt on this branch to "show the model the draft and ask what it thinks".
Chapter III · the obstacle

Where the draft tokens actually go

Not just before the judged slot, and not everywhere. It alternates.

Every slot is masked in exactly one of the two forwards, and read there. Why not fill all 31 neighbours instead? That would give each judged slot the full draft as context, but at 32 forwards per block rather than 2. The price of the cheap version is that a judged slot only ever sees half the draft.

Which leaves the conclusion the whole project rests on: the ability "judge whether this token belongs in this slot, given its neighbours" does not exist anywhere in the frozen model. There is nothing to extract, so no threshold, vote or prompt can extract it. If we want it, we put it there.

Chapter V · the method

Why attention is the right thing to edit

Both behaviours the judge needs already exist in the network. Nothing new has to be learned from scratch.

  • "Copy the token at my own slot" is an identity attention pattern — that is what fires at filled slots today.
  • "Infer a slot from its neighbours" is the masked-slot machinery SDAR spent 50 B tokens training.
  • The adapter's whole job is to make the second fire where the first currently does. That is a change in where attention looks — exactly what a low-rank update to q, k, v, o expresses.
This is why 27 M parameters is a plausible budget for undoing a habit inherited from trillions of pretraining tokens. We are not teaching a new skill; we are re-routing between two the model already has.

Chapter V · the method

The loss: say the right token, and be sure about it

L = meanₖ ∈ ₒ [ −log pᵢ(Tᵢ) ] cross-entropy, every open slot + β · meanₖ ∈ ℂ H( softmax(zᵢ / τ) ) sharpen, ONLY where already right ℂ = { i ∈ ₒ : argmax pᵢ = Tᵢ } β = 2.0 τ = 0.5 [MASK] column removed

AdamW, lr 2e−5, cosine, global batch 32 states, 1,057 steps = two epochs over 16,905 blocks. 5.3 s per step on 8 GPUs.

  • Term one teaches what to say. Cross-entropy against the block's final tokens.
  • Term two attacks the bottleneck. A third of slots are right but under 0.95. Where the top guess is already the target, push it toward 1 so the slot clears the threshold in one pass instead of three.
  • Never applied to a wrong slot. It sharpens a correct distribution; it cannot entrench a wrong one.
  • [MASK] is deleted before the entropy. Otherwise entropy could be minimised by piling probability on a token that is never a legal output.
Honest about scale. ~600,000 supervised slots, 7 to 15× less than dParallel used for a comparable behaviour change. Under-confidence was flagged in advance as the likeliest failure. It is exactly what happened.
Chapter VI · results

Round 1 lost to a policy with no parameters at all

The diagnostic is one comparison. There is a trivial policy available to any judge: just copy whatever token is already in the slot. It scores 0.556, because that is the drafter's agreement rate. Round 1 scored 0.393.

The part that stings. At inference we know which slots are drafts — the decoder just placed them there. Every speculative-decoding verifier is told which tokens are speculative; every diffusion model is told its noise level. Round 1 withheld free information and then asked the model to infer it from tokens designed to look plausible.
Chapter VI · results

Round 2a: same data, same 1,057 steps, the flag added

The repair is unambiguous at the level of the mechanism. Same corpus, same budget, trained from scratch — so the gain belongs to the flag and the gate, not to more compute.

  • 0.393 → 0.579 at draft slots, finally above the copy baseline.
  • 77% → 96% precision on confident verdicts.
  • All-mask decoding never moved. Pinned at the stock 0.532 at all ten evaluations — the gate makes that a theorem, not a hope.
  • End to end: 38% → 94%.
And the tell for what is still missing. The judge is right when it is confident, but it is only confident at 39% of slots — and that share was still rising when the cosine schedule decayed to zero. It is under-trained, exactly as the data-budget arithmetic predicted.
Chapter VI · results

The decode loop: commit, keep, or re-mask

One 30B pass per round, with every open slot holding the draft and the flag armed there. Then a three-way verdict per slot, and repeat.

  • Commit to the judge's argmax if it beats τ. Never revisited.
  • Keep tentative — leave the draft token in place as context — if the judge's argmax agrees with it but is not confident enough.
  • Re-mask if the judge's argmax disagrees.
The middle branch is the contribution. A stock decoder has no such state: an uncommitted slot is blank. Here a slot the model half-believes stays readable to its neighbours, so the next pass conditions on it instead of on nothing.

Chapter VII · where we stand

One level down: it has two kinds of decision, and only one is safe

Split every confident commit in two. A confirmation agrees with the drafter. An override overrules it. On 8,687 held-out pass-1 slots the two behave nothing alike.

Why it ties. Confirmations cannot exceed the drafter's 55.5%, and the judge is confident on only 62% of the correct drafts it sees. The decisions that would break that ceiling are the overrides, the 17%-wrong ones. Two caps, and the model is pinned between them.
Chapter VII · where we stand

The errors are single tokens in the answer wrapper

The judge's prose is indistinguishable from the stock model's — the same reasoning steps, occasionally a different word. The failures are not sloppy thinking. They are one bad override in the last few tokens.

stock → \boxed{180}$. judge → \boxed{180$. missing brace stock → $\boxed{64}$ judge → boxed{64}$ missing backslash

Correct reasoning, correct number, wrong wrapper, marked wrong. At τ = .95, 2 or 3 of the 9 wrong answers are this. Under confirm .90 / override .99 they vanish entirely.

Encouraging, not damning. A model that broke the reasoning would have broken judgement. A model that breaks a brace has broken calibration at a handful of high-stakes slots. That is a threshold and data problem.

On a task where one token flips the score, override precision matters far more than average token accuracy. The asymmetric rule exists because of this.

Stock and the free rule never break the format. They only fill slots the big model was already sure of. The judge is the first decoder that replaces a token, and replacement is where the risk lives.
Chapter VIII · what happens next

The hypothesis: it is under-trained, not wrong

"Train it more" is usually a bad answer. Here there is specific evidence for it, and it is worth being precise about what that evidence is and what it is not.

the case for
  • Nothing had plateaued. Accuracy, confident share and precision were all still climbing at step 1,057, and accuracy was accelerating.
  • Not a learning-rate artefact. The schedule only decayed to half of peak, so it was still learning when the run ended.
  • The corpus was known to be too small beforehand. ~600,000 supervised slots against dParallel's 4–9 million. Round 2b takes it to ~2.3×.
  • The bottleneck is recall, not correctness. Confirmations are already 99% precise. The judge is simply not sure often enough.
What that buys. Confident slots 0.39 → ~0.52 lifts confirmations from 35% to ~43% of open slots: capturing 78% of the available correct drafts instead of 62%. About 3 more commits per pass, at 99% precision.

Chapter VIII · how good a bet is this

The two things that would most change my mind

what is missing, and it is our fault
  • The no-draft control has never run. Same recipe, draft disabled, certainty forcing alone. If it matches the draft arm, the draft adds nothing. We cannot rule that out today.
  • The trained judge has never seen 500 items. Every trained number here carries a ±5-point interval. The frozen cell is queued but has not run.
Bluntly. The strongest supportable claim today: a 27 M-parameter adapter teaches a diffusion LLM to read its own filled slots, 38% → 94% end to end, landing level with a rule that costs nothing. Everything past that is a hypothesis with a schedule attached.
what would make me raise the odds
  • Confident share past 0.45 by step 2,000 with precision holding above 0.96. That is on-trend and would mean the recall lever is real rather than extrapolated.
  • Override precision above 0.90 at τ = 0.95. That single number is what currently forces the strict threshold that gives the pass savings back.
  • Drafter agreement past 0.65. It raises the confirmation ceiling for free and makes every later judge round easier.
One cheap thing worth doing regardless. Deploy the draft-informed expert pool. It attacks cost per pass, so it can rescue a partial result: if passes stall at 7 but each touches 32 experts instead of 58, the cost-per-token claim survives on its own.
Chapter VIII · the honest position

What is settled, what is not, and what the paper is either way

settled
  • The mechanism findings. Filled slots return the next input token. Draft context makes the model sycophantic. The empty-block marginal is the only honest free signal.
  • The free rule is held out. +0.2 [−1.4, +1.8] points, 16% fewer passes, 500 unseen items.
  • Flag-and-gate is the right fix. 0.393 → 0.579 and 38% → 94%, at identical data and budget.

Chapter IX · round 3, the drafter

Same objective it was born with. Different teacher.

The drafter is itself a block-diffusion model, built from Qwen3-1.7B with 50 B tokens of continued pretraining and 4 B tokens of supervised fine-tuning, both under the objective below. We do not invent a new one. We change y.

own answer to the same problem, kept only when correct Hard-label sequence distillation: the student trains on the teacher's own samples (Kim & Rush 2016). All 1.7 B parameters move.
Why this is the standard move. Speculative decoding always trains its drafter on the target model's outputs. What is unusual here is only that the target is a diffusion model, so the objective the drafter already has happens to be exactly the right one to reuse.
